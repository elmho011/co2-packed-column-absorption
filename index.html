CO2 absorption in a 4-inch packed column: pressure drop, KOGa and the effect of NaOH

We measured gas-side pressure drop over 18 gas and liquid rate combinations and absorbed CO2 into water and into dilute NaOH. This page compares the hydraulics with the generalized pressure drop correlation (GPDC), reports the overall gas-phase mass-transfer coefficient for CO2 into 0.076 M NaOH, and explains why the hydroxide controls the driving force.

Objective 1

The GPDC under-predicts pressure drop in this column

For each run on 17 Sep we averaged the logged pressure drop, divided by the 4 ft bed height, and read the Strigle version of the GPDC (Perry's Fig. 14-48, digitized in GPDC-1.xlsx) at the run's flow parameter and capacity parameter. Measured ΔP/Z is higher than predicted at every one of the 18 settings. Where the point falls inside the chart, the excess runs from 26% to 142%, with a median of 75%. The predictions closely reproduce the values in the lab group's own "GPDC Comparison" sheet (mean difference 6%), so the gap comes from the column itself, not from the arithmetic.

C_s = U_s·[ρ_G/(ρ_L − ρ_G)]^0.5 capacity parameter CP = C_s·F_p^0.5·ν^0.05 F_LV = (L/G)·(ρ_G/ρ_L)^0.5 U_s in ft/s, F_p = 200 ft⁻¹, ν = 0.96 cSt ΔP/Z (GPDC) = curve value at (F_LV, CP), log-interpolated between the 0.05 … 1.5 in H2O/ft curves

Why the measured ΔP runs high
  • High flow parameter. 13 of 18 runs have FLV > 0.3. Kister et al. (2007) report that the GPDC is optimistic, meaning it predicts too little ΔP, above FLV ≈ 0.3. It also loses accuracy at low liquid rates.
  • Small column, small packing. The column-to-packing diameter ratio is only 8. Wall channelling and a non-uniform bed are expected, and published Fp values come from much larger towers. Kister et al. recommend GPDC interpolation charts built from data for the specific packing for exactly this reason.
  • What the taps measure. If the pressure taps span the support plate and liquid distributor as well as the bed, the measurement includes losses that the per-foot correlation does not.
  • Early loading. At 1.5 gpm, ΔP/Z jumped from 0.62 to 1.34 in H2O/ft between 8 and 9 SCFM, while the GPDC predicted only 0.49 to 0.71. Perry's §14 treats 1.5 in H2O/ft as incipient flooding. The 9 SCFM point is close to that, about 2 SCFM below the GPDC flood estimate of 11 SCFM.
Objective 2

KOGa for CO2 into 0.076 M NaOH is about 20 kmol/(h·m³·atm)

Because the hydroxide reacts with CO2 quickly and stays in excess, the liquid holds essentially no free CO2, so y* ≈ 0 throughout the bed. With only about 3% CO2 the system is dilute, so the integrated rate equation reduces to Perry's Eq. 14-76:

N_OG = ln(y_in / y_out) H_OG = Z / N_OG K_OG·a = G_M / (H_OG · P) G_M = 65.1 kmol/(h·m²) (7.5 SCFM at 70 °F, 14.7 psia over 0.0873 ft²) Z = 1.219 m P = 1 atm

The CO2 sensors needed a correction first. With no CO2 fed (17 Sep) and in every water run, the outlet analyzer read higher than the inlet analyzer, by 6–10% in the water runs. Water cannot produce that: at these flows its absorption factor is only about 0.01–0.03, so it can remove at most 1–3% of the CO2. The difference is therefore an offset between the two instruments. A straight-line fit of outlet reading against inlet reading over those 27 runs gives out = 0.047 + 1.068·in. Inverting this line puts the NaOH outlet readings on the inlet analyzer's scale.

How it compares

Our corrected 19.9 kmol/(h·m³·atm) is well below Perry's 1 N values (45.1 for 25 mm ceramic Intalox, 38 for 38 mm). That is expected at 0.076 M: CO2 absorption into NaOH is liquid-film controlled (Perry's p. 14-18), and the reaction-enhanced liquid coefficient grows only with √[OH⁻]. Scaling the 25 mm value to our mean [OH⁻] of 0.061 M gives about 12. Our ½-in saddles carry 2.4× the surface area per volume of 25 mm saddles (623 vs 256 m²/m³, Table 14-7b), so a result somewhat above 12 is consistent with the literature. The back-calculated effective interfacial area is 184 m²/m³, about 30% of the dry packing area.

Objective 3

NaOH keeps the driving force at its maximum, and its concentration sets how fast the column uses it

The driving force for absorption is y − y*, the gap between the CO2 in the gas and the CO2 that would be in equilibrium with the liquid. In water, dissolved CO2 builds up as the liquid descends: y* climbs toward y and the driving force pinches near the bottom. At our flows water has an absorption factor A = L/(mG) of only 0.020, so it saturates after removing about 1.7% of the CO2. Hydroxide converts dissolved CO2 to carbonate (CO2 + 2 OH⁻ → CO3²⁻ + H2O). Free CO2 stays near zero, so y* ≈ 0 and the whole gas-phase concentration is available as driving force, from the bottom to the top of the bed.

Hydroxide concentration then sets the rate. With k₂ = 6,676 L/(mol·s) at 22 °C and a mean [OH⁻] of 0.061 M, the Hatta number is about 9. That is well below the instantaneous-reaction limit E∞ ≈ 94, so the reaction is fast pseudo-first-order: absorption is enhanced by E ≈ √(1 + Ha²), which scales with √[OH⁻]. A second limit is stoichiometric. Each mole of CO2 consumes two moles of OH⁻, so complete removal at these flows needs at least 0.093 M NaOH. At 0.076 M we converted about 38% of the hydroxide, leaving the bottom of the bed at about 0.047 M.

Driving force along the bed: water vs 0.076 M NaOH (same gas and liquid rates)

NaOH: measured NOG = 0.373 with y* = 0. Water: countercurrent integration with Henry's law (H ≈ 1,630 atm) and the physical coefficient KOGa = 2.24 kmol/(h·m³·atm), the NaOH value divided by the enhancement factor. The water curve is a prediction, because the analyzers cannot resolve a 2% change. At the top, fresh liquid enters, so y* = 0 for both liquids. Water's curve is higher there only because it has removed almost no CO2 from the gas. Lower in the bed, its y* climbs until little driving force is left.

Predicted KOGa vs feed NaOH concentration

Model: E = √(1 + Ha²), with Ha ∝ √[OH⁻], anchored at our measured point. Above about 0.5 N, ionic-strength and viscosity effects (Perry's Fig. 14-12) would flatten the real curve, so treat that range as indicative only.

Uncertainty

Error sources and how large they are

  • Analyzer offset, the largest effect. The correction raises KOGa from 15.2 to 19.9. Water runs remove up to 1–3% of the CO2, which slightly inflates the fitted slope, so the corrected value may be high by a similar few percent.
  • Pressure and temperature at the analyzers. NDIR sensors count molecules per volume, so readings scale with p/T (Vaisala). A 2.2 in H2O pressure difference between inlet and outlet changes the reading by about 0.5%. A 10 °C temperature difference changes it by about 3%.
  • Humidity. Gas leaving the bed is close to saturation (about 2.6% H2O at 22 °C), which dilutes the outlet CO2 reading by about 2–3% if the inlet gas is dry (Vaisala Table 5). The cross-calibration absorbs this, because the water runs were humidified the same way.
  • Rotameters. Variable-area meters are typically ±3% of full scale (Mosedale), so percentage errors are largest at the low end, such as 3 SCFM and 0.5 gpm. Gas containing 3% CO2 is denser than the air the meter was calibrated for, so it reads about 0.8% high (correction √(Mair/M)).
  • Assumed properties. Taking T = 22 °C sets ν, ρ, Henry's constant and k₂. ±3 °C moves k₂ by about ±20%, but KOGa comes from the measured compositions and does not depend on it.
  • Transients. Start-up (0–60 s) and the liquid-supply loss after 680 s are excluded. Pump start and stop transients (Perry's §6, hydraulic transients) disturb flow briefly.
  • Once-through NaOH. At 1.35 gpm the steady window used about 53 L of solution. The calculation assumes 0.076 M was the feed concentration throughout. If the solution was recirculated, the hydroxide conversion figure would understate depletion.
